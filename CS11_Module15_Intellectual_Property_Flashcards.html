AnalyzeTask A: Is Copying a Song Really Theft?

The reading presents two competing views on digital copying. Break the debate down: identify the key claim, the evidence or reasoning, and the underlying assumption of each side. Then explain where the two sides actually disagree.

View 1: "Copying is theft"View 2: "The system is unfair"
Creators invest years of study and training. When the public copies and distributes their work, creators lose sales, can't earn a living, and may leave their careers, so "creative capital" withers. Copying a file leaves the original intact. Intellectual property is often overpriced, and record and movie companies keep most of the profit while creators get a small percentage. Some musicians now sell directly to fans.
0 words (aim for 60+)
Expert analysis:
  • View 1 claims that unauthorized copying is theft. Its evidence is lost future revenue. Its assumption is that every pirated copy replaces a sale, and that income is what motivates creators to keep creating.
  • View 2 claims that copying isn't like stealing a physical item. Its evidence is that the original file is unchanged and that profits are unevenly split. Its assumption is that the harm of piracy falls mainly on corporations, not on creators.
  • The real disagreement isn't about whether a file is physically taken. Both sides agree it isn't. It's about how to measure harm (lost potential revenue vs. lost physical property) and who is harmed (creators vs. the companies that distribute their work).
  • A key point: View 2 criticizes the business model, but that doesn't make piracy legal. Unauthorized copying and distribution is still copyright infringement. Some artists' response, selling directly to fans, is a legal alternative.

EvaluateTask B: The Ebook That Won't Open

Scenario: Alicia legally bought a DRM-protected ebook for her CS11 class. She just got a new tablet, and the ebook won't open on it. A friend sends her a free tool that strips DRM and converts the book to a PDF. Evaluate her options and recommend one. Justify your recommendation using the law, security risk, and fairness to the publisher.
0 words (aim for 50+)
Expert evaluation: Option 2 is the best choice.
  • Option 1 is illegal, even though she paid. The DMCA makes it a crime to circumvent DRM, and it outlaws software designed to crack DRM. Owning a legal copy doesn't give her the right to break its protection. Free cracking tools from unknown sources are also a common way to spread malware.
  • Option 3 is piracy. File-sharing networks are "rife with malware, adware, and hackers," and downloading the book violates the publisher's copyright.
  • Option 2 respects the law and the publisher's rights, carries no security risk, and usually solves the problem, because DRM typically limits which devices and apps can open a file, so the approved app will work.
  • A fair point of criticism: Alicia's frustration is exactly why DRM is unpopular and why groups like Defective by Design campaign against it. Disagreeing with a law is a reason to advocate for change, not to break it.

CreateTask C: Write a Usage License for Your Own Work

Scenario: You've built a portfolio of original photos of Stockton landmarks (the Delta campus, the waterfront, Miracle Mile) and posted them online. Inspired by Creative Commons, which lets owners "more finely describe what can and cannot be done with their work," write your own short usage license. Then explain why you made each choice.

Your license should decide, with a reason for each:

  • Must people give you credit (attribution)? How?
  • May people use the photos commercially, for example in ads or on products for sale?
  • May people edit or remix the photos? If so, must they share their versions under the same terms?

Self-assess your license against this checklist:

0 of 6 criteria met
Model license (one strong possibility):

"Stockton Landmarks" photos © 2026 Jordan Lee. You MAY share these photos and edit them (crop, filter, add text) for personal, school, and nonprofit projects. You MUST credit me as "Photo by Jordan Lee" with a link to my portfolio, and you must share any edited versions under these same terms. You may NOT use these photos in advertising or on products you sell without my written permission.

  • Attribution required: my goal is exposure for my photography career, so credit and a link bring new visitors.
  • No commercial use: if a business wants my work, I want to be paid. This keeps a potential income stream.
  • Edits allowed, with share-alike: remixes spread my work further, and share-alike keeps the same rules attached to every version.

These choices match the real Creative Commons BY-NC-SA (Attribution–NonCommercial–ShareAlike) license.
